Air purifiers are no longer a winter-only purchase in India. Dust, construction, traffic and crop-burning smoke push indoor PM2.5 well above safe levels for months at a time in many cities, and a closed room does not protect you on its own. A good purifier is one of the few things that measurably cleans the air you breathe at home.

The hard part is choosing one. Box claims like "covers 900 sq ft" or "removes 99.99% of viruses" make every model sound the same. They are not. What matters is how much clean air the purifier actually moves, how big your room really is, and what the filters will cost you every year after you buy it.

This guide covers six purifiers that are on sale in India now, at very different sizes and budgets. For each one we explain who it suits, what it does well, what it does badly, and who should skip it.

## How to pick the right size: ignore the box, use CADR

CADR, or clean air delivery rate, is the volume of filtered air a purifier delivers per hour, measured in m³/h (some US-style listings use CFM). It is the single most useful number on the spec sheet, because everything else follows from it.

The "covers up to X sq ft" claim on the box is usually calculated at two air changes per hour. That means the purifier replaces the air in the room twice an hour. That is fine for a light allergy season. It is far too slow for a December evening in Delhi-NCR, when outdoor PM2.5 can sit in the hundreds and leaks in through every gap around doors and windows.

A more useful target for Indian conditions is:

- **Five air changes per hour** for winter smog, crop-burning season or a room where someone has asthma or a respiratory condition
- **Three air changes per hour** for everyday dust and pollen in cleaner months

You can work out the CADR your room needs in three steps:

1. Convert the floor area to square metres by multiplying square feet by 0.093.
2. Multiply by the ceiling height in metres (about 3 m for a 10 ft ceiling) to get the room volume.
3. Multiply by 5 (or 3) to get the CADR you need in m³/h.

Here is what that looks like for common Indian room sizes with a 10 ft ceiling:

| Room size | Needed for heavy smog (5 ACH) | Needed for everyday use (3 ACH) |
|---|---|---|
| 120 sq ft (small bedroom) | about 170 m³/h | about 100 m³/h |
| 150 sq ft (standard bedroom) | about 210 m³/h | about 125 m³/h |
| 200 sq ft (master bedroom) | about 280 m³/h | about 170 m³/h |
| 300 sq ft (living room) | about 420 m³/h | about 250 m³/h |
| 400 sq ft (large hall) | about 560 m³/h | about 335 m³/h |

If you are between two sizes, buy the larger purifier and run it on a lower, quieter speed. A big purifier on low is almost always quieter than a small one on full speed. For open-plan homes, one purifier will not clean the whole flat. Put it in the room where you sleep, because that is where you spend the most continuous hours.

The sticker price is only part of the cost. Filters are the real long-term expense, and in polluted Indian cities they wear out faster than the rated figures suggest.

- **Philips AC0920:** FY0900/30 filter, rated for up to a year, about ₹2,400 on Philips' own store
- **Philips AC0950:** FY0910/30 filter, roughly ₹3,000 to ₹3,500, with Philips suggesting replacement every 6 to 12 months depending on use
- **Honeywell Air Touch V1:** about 9,000 hours
- **Qubo Q600:** up to 15,000 hours
- **Mijia Smart Air Purifier 6:** about one year, depending on air quality

In a heavily polluted city, plan for one main filter a year whatever the rating says. A clogged filter does not fail in an obvious way. It simply moves less air, so the purifier gets louder and cleans less. If your room never gets back to a "good" reading on auto mode, the filter is the first thing to check.

**Electricity.** Purifiers use little power. As an upper limit, running each one at full rated power for 10 hours a day at ₹8 per unit costs about:

- **Honeywell Air Touch V1 (12 W):** about ₹350 a year
- **Philips AC0920 or AC0950 (21 W):** about ₹610 a year
- **Coway AirMega 150 (35 W):** about ₹1,020 a year

Real bills are lower, because purifiers spend most of their time on lower speeds in auto mode. Xiaomi and Qubo do not prominently publish power figures for these models, but both use efficient DC motors, and reviewers describe their power draw as modest.

## Features that matter, and ones you can ignore

**Worth paying for:**

- **True HEPA (H13) filtration.** This is the core of any good purifier. Be wary of vague terms like "HEPA-type" or "HEPA-like".
- **An air quality sensor and auto mode.** This saves you from guessing. The purifier speeds up when pollution rises and goes quiet when the air is clean.
- **A quiet sleep mode with a dimmable display.** You will not run a purifier overnight if it keeps you awake.
- **A filter change indicator.** It is easy to forget a filter for two years.
- **Activated carbon,** if you want help with smells. Thicker carbon is better, and a formaldehyde CADR rating (as the Mijia 6 has) shows it has been tested.

**Nice to have:**

- **An app and Wi-Fi.** Useful for schedules and for switching the purifier on before you get home, and some apps show outdoor AQI alongside indoor readings.
- **PM1 sensing.** PM1 particles are smaller than PM2.5. Seeing them is interesting, but it does not change what the HEPA filter does.

**Safe to ignore:**

- **"Kills 99.99% of viruses" claims.** These lab results come from controlled test chambers, not living rooms. HEPA filters capture airborne particles well, but no purifier makes a room sterile.
- **Ionisers and UV-C lights.** They add little to a good HEPA filter. If yours has a switch, turning them off costs you almost nothing.
- **Huge coverage numbers.** Go by CADR and the room-size table above.

## Getting the most out of your purifier

- **Close the room.** A purifier cleans the air in a room, not the whole house. With a door or window open, it is trying to clean the outdoors.
- **Give it space.** Keep it at least 30 to 50 cm from walls and furniture on the sides where air goes in, and do not tuck it behind a sofa or under a desk.
- **Place it where you breathe.** In a bedroom, a spot a couple of metres from the bed works well. Close enough to help, far enough that the airflow is not in your face.
- **Run it before you need it.** Switch it on 30 to 60 minutes before bedtime, or use the app or a schedule, so the room is already clean when you lie down.
- **Leave it on auto.** In smoggy months, letting it run continuously on auto mode is more effective than short bursts on high.
- **Clean the pre-filter.** Vacuum or wash it (if the manual allows) every two to four weeks in dusty months. It protects the expensive HEPA filter behind it.
- **Use the right tool for cooking.** Purifiers help with lingering smoke, but a kitchen chimney or exhaust fan removes cooking fumes at the source far better.

If anyone in your home has asthma, COPD or another breathing condition, a purifier can be part of reducing exposure, but it is not a medical treatment. Talk to your doctor about what else you should be doing on high-pollution days.
