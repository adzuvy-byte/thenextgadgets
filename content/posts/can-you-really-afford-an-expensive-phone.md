Can you really afford an expensive phone, or can you simply find a way to pay for one? There is a big difference. With flagship smartphones increasingly crossing ₹1 lakh and EMIs making premium devices feel easier to buy, it's tempting to judge affordability by the monthly payment rather than the actual cost. But a smartphone is only truly affordable when buying it doesn't interfere with your savings, investments, essential expenses, debt payments, or other financial priorities. Before spending heavily on your next phone, it's worth looking beyond the price tag and asking what the purchase will actually cost you.

Buying an expensive phone is easier than ever. You can pay with a credit card, split the cost into monthly installments, trade in your old device, use a bank offer and walk out with a ₹1 lakh smartphone without feeling like you just spent ₹1 lakh. But **can you afford an expensive phone** is a very different question from whether a bank, credit-card company or retailer will let you buy one.

That distinction matters.

A premium smartphone can be an excellent purchase when you use its camera, performance, display, battery life or software support enough to justify the price. But a phone can also become one of the easiest ways to turn a comfortable income into unnecessary monthly commitments.

And the problem isn't limited to people buying the most expensive iPhone or Android flagship. A ₹50,000 phone can be financially uncomfortable for one person and completely reasonable for another.

The right question isn't simply, "How much does the phone cost?"

It is:

**How much does this phone cost relative to everything else I need my money to do?**

## The Smartphone Market Has Become More Expensive

Premium smartphones have become a much larger part of the Indian market. Counterpoint Research reported that India's smartphone market grew 8% in value during 2025 despite only 1% growth in shipments, with premiumization playing a major role. The ultra-premium segment above ₹45,000 also reached a record 17% share of smartphone shipments in Q4 2025.

At the same time, phones are getting more expensive.

Counterpoint reported that the average launch price of smartphones introduced in India during Q1 2026 increased 11% year over year to ₹37,200. Rising memory costs were one of the factors pushing manufacturers toward higher prices.

Apple's recent pricing changes provide an extreme example. In September 2026, the company raised prices on existing iPhones in India, with the iPhone 17 starting at ₹99,900 after a substantial increase. Reuters attributed the global pricing pressure partly to higher memory and storage costs.

So the question of smartphone affordability is becoming more relevant, not less.

But salary alone doesn't tell you whether a phone is affordable.

## The Biggest Mistake: Confusing Income With Affordability

Suppose two people earn ₹1 lakh per month.

Person A:

- Rent: ₹25,000
- Household expenses: ₹20,000
- Existing EMI: ₹15,000
- Insurance and investments: ₹10,000
- Family responsibilities: ₹15,000
- Remaining: ₹15,000

Person B:

- Rent: ₹10,000
- Household expenses: ₹15,000
- No existing EMI
- Insurance and investments: ₹15,000
- Family responsibilities: ₹5,000
- Remaining: ₹55,000

Both earn the same salary.

But recommending them the same phone budget would make little sense.

Person A might technically be able to purchase a ₹1 lakh phone. Person B has substantially more financial flexibility.

That's why **take-home income is only the starting point**.

Your real phone budget should come from your disposable cash flow after essential expenses, existing debt, savings and financial commitments.

## Don't Ask "Can I Buy It?" Ask "Can I Comfortably Own It?"

This is the more useful test.

There are actually three different questions:

### 1. Can I buy it?

You have enough money, credit limit or financing eligibility to complete the transaction.

### 2. Can I pay for it?

You can make the monthly EMI or credit-card payment without missing it.

### 3. Can I comfortably afford it?

You can purchase the phone without sacrificing emergency savings, investments, essential expenses or other meaningful financial goals.

The third question is the one that matters.

A person earning ₹80,000 per month might be approved for a ₹1 lakh phone on EMI. That doesn't automatically make the phone affordable.

## The EMI Trap

EMIs are useful.

They can make a large purchase easier to manage, especially when the purchase is necessary and the financing terms are genuinely favorable.

But EMI also changes how expensive something feels.

A ₹1,20,000 phone can sound intimidating.

"Only ₹10,000 a month for 12 months" sounds considerably easier.

The total amount hasn't changed.

Only the psychological presentation has.

This is one reason expensive phones can quietly become lifestyle inflation. Recent reporting on smartphone spending in India has highlighted the same phenomenon: people can normalize premium devices because monthly payments make them appear more accessible.

### EMI affordability is not the same as purchase affordability

Consider a hypothetical ₹1,20,000 phone.

At 12 months:

**₹1,20,000 ÷ 12 = ₹10,000 per month**

Now imagine that you already have:

- ₹8,000 car EMI
- ₹5,000 personal loan EMI
- ₹3,000 credit-card payment
- ₹10,000 phone EMI

That's ₹26,000 going toward debt or installment commitments before considering rent, food, utilities, transportation and savings.

The phone didn't become cheaper because it became an EMI.

You simply moved the spending into the future.

## A Better Way to Set Your Phone Budget

There is no universal percentage of salary that everyone should spend on a phone.

Instead, use a **three-layer test**.

### Layer 1: Essential expenses

Calculate what you need every month:

- Housing
- Food
- Utilities
- Transportation
- Insurance
- Medical expenses
- Family commitments
- Existing loan payments

These expenses come first.

### Layer 2: Financial foundations

Next, account for:

- Emergency savings
- Retirement investments
- Long-term investments
- Short-term financial goals
- Insurance premiums
- Planned major expenses

If buying the phone means stopping your SIP, emptying your emergency fund or delaying an important financial goal, the phone is probably too expensive for you.

### Layer 3: Lifestyle spending

Only after the first two layers should you consider discretionary purchases:

- Smartphone
- Laptop
- Watch
- Headphones
- Gaming console
- Holidays
- Restaurants
- Entertainment

Your phone belongs here unless it is genuinely required for your livelihood.

## Try the "Three-Month Test"

Here's a simple test that is more useful than a generic salary rule.

Suppose the phone costs ₹90,000.

Before buying it, put aside an amount equivalent to the planned monthly phone expense for three months.

If you plan to spend ₹7,500 per month, save ₹7,500 for three consecutive months.

If doing that feels effortless, your cash flow may comfortably support the purchase.

If you repeatedly have to dip into savings or skip other expenses, you've learned something important:

**The phone may be affordable on paper but uncomfortable in real life.**

This test also removes some of the temptation created by EMI marketing.

You're proving to yourself that the payment fits your lifestyle before committing to the purchase.

## What About the "One Month Salary" Rule?

You may have heard advice such as:

> Don't spend more than one month's salary on a phone.

It's easy to understand, but it's not a financial law.

For someone earning ₹40,000 per month, spending ₹40,000 on a phone could still be excessive if that person has debt and no emergency savings.

For someone earning ₹3 lakh per month with substantial savings and very low fixed expenses, a ₹1 lakh phone could be relatively insignificant.

So don't blindly use salary as the denominator.

**Use disposable income and financial obligations instead.**

Salary tells you how much comes in.

Cash flow tells you how much freedom you actually have.

## When an Expensive Phone Can Be Justified

A ₹1 lakh smartphone isn't automatically a bad purchase.

There are legitimate reasons to spend more.

### You're a photographer or videographer

If the phone is regularly used to produce paid content, better cameras, stabilization, microphones, storage and processing can have genuine economic value.

In this case, the phone isn't simply entertainment.

It's equipment.

### You're a creator

Someone producing YouTube videos, Instagram Reels, product photography or social-media content may benefit substantially from a better camera system and stronger processing.

The important question is whether those capabilities are actually being used.

Buying a flagship because you *might* start creating content isn't the same as buying one because you already produce content every week.

### Your phone is a work tool

For some professionals, the smartphone is essentially a portable computer.

If you spend several hours every day handling business communication, editing documents, managing clients, taking photographs, conducting video calls or running applications, paying more for reliability and performance can make sense.

### You keep phones for many years

This is one of the strongest arguments for buying a premium device.

A ₹90,000 phone kept for five years costs:

**₹18,000 per year**

A ₹45,000 phone replaced every two years costs:

**₹22,500 per year**

The cheaper phone isn't automatically the cheaper ownership decision.

Longevity matters.

However, this only works if you actually keep the phone for five years. Buying a new flagship every 18 months destroys much of the argument.

## When an Expensive Phone Probably Isn't Worth It

There are also some obvious warning signs.

### You're buying it mainly for status

There's nothing inherently wrong with enjoying luxury products.

But be honest about the reason.

If you primarily want the phone because people around you have one, social media makes it look desirable or you want to signal success, you're paying for an emotional benefit rather than technological necessity.

That doesn't make the purchase immoral.

It does mean you should evaluate it as a luxury purchase rather than pretending it is a financial necessity.

### You're financing it for too long

A smartphone is a depreciating electronic device.

Stretching payments over an extended period can create an uncomfortable situation where you're still paying for a phone after its resale value has dropped dramatically.

### You have no emergency fund

If buying the phone would leave you with almost no accessible savings, don't do it.

A flagship phone won't help when an unexpected medical bill, job interruption or urgent family expense appears.

### You're carrying credit-card debt

This is an especially important warning sign.

Paying high-interest credit-card debt while purchasing a premium smartphone is generally difficult to justify financially.

The phone can wait.

Expensive interest usually shouldn't.

### You're upgrading every year

Annual upgrades can be enjoyable, but they are rarely necessary.

Modern flagship phones are already extremely capable. For many users, upgrading every two or three years produces a much better value proposition than replacing the device every year.

## The "I Deserve It" Argument

This one deserves more attention.

After working hard for a year, it's natural to want something nice.

And sometimes you should buy it.

The problem begins when "I deserve it" becomes the justification for every expensive purchase.

A better approach is:

**I worked hard, and I've already taken care of my financial priorities. Now I can enjoy this purchase without feeling guilty about it.**

There's a huge difference.

A luxury purchase becomes much more enjoyable when it doesn't create financial anxiety afterward.

## What If You Can Afford the EMI But Not the Full Price?

This is where you need to be careful.

If you have enough cash to buy the phone outright but choose a genuinely no-cost EMI because you prefer keeping your money invested or liquid, the calculation can be reasonable.

That's different from needing an EMI because you don't have enough money to purchase the device.

The first is a financing decision.

The second is a potential affordability problem.

Never let the availability of an EMI determine your phone budget.

**Set the budget first. Then decide how to pay.**

Not the other way around.

## Don't Forget the Cost Beyond the Phone

The sticker price isn't necessarily the entire cost.

Depending on the device, you may also spend money on:

- Case
- Screen protector
- Charger
- Power adapter
- Wireless charger
- Earbuds
- Cloud storage
- Extended warranty
- Insurance
- Premium accessories
- Repairs
- Data or service plans

None of these individually changes the decision much.

Together, they can add a meaningful amount.

A ₹90,000 phone can easily become a ₹95,000–₹1 lakh ownership purchase once accessories and protection are included.

## Resale Value Can Change the Calculation

Don't ignore resale value either.

Suppose:

**Phone A costs ₹1,00,000 and sells for ₹40,000 after three years.**

Your net ownership cost is:

**₹60,000**

Now consider:

**Phone B costs ₹60,000 and sells for ₹15,000 after three years.**

Your net ownership cost is:

**₹45,000**

Phone B is still cheaper.

But the gap isn't ₹40,000 anymore.

This is why comparing purchase prices alone can produce misleading conclusions.

When evaluating premium phones, look at:

**Purchase price − realistic resale value = effective ownership cost**

Don't use inflated exchange values or promotional trade-in numbers unless you're confident you can actually obtain them.

## A ₹1 Lakh Phone Isn't Automatically Better Value Than a ₹50,000 Phone

This is another important distinction.

A premium phone can be better.

That doesn't necessarily mean it is twice as valuable.

A ₹1 lakh flagship might give you:

- Better cameras
- Better materials
- A brighter display
- Faster processor
- Longer software support
- Better water resistance
- More advanced connectivity
- Better speakers
- More storage
- More consistent performance

But you need to identify which of those differences matter to you.

If you mostly:

- WhatsApp
- Instagram
- YouTube
- Google Maps
- Banking
- Calls
- Web browsing
- Streaming

then you may receive relatively little practical benefit from doubling your phone budget.

The more expensive device may still be nicer.

But **nicer and better value are not the same thing.**

## The 80% Rule Is More Useful Than Chasing the Best Phone

Here's a practical way to think about smartphone purchases.

Find the phone that delivers roughly **80–90% of what you want** without forcing you into financial discomfort.

The last 10–20% of capability can be disproportionately expensive.

For example, moving from a basic phone to a good mid-range phone can dramatically improve the experience.

Moving from a good mid-range phone to a flagship can also produce meaningful improvements.

But moving from one flagship to the absolute top model may deliver much smaller everyday benefits relative to the price difference.

That's diminishing returns.

And smartphones are particularly susceptible to it.

## Ask These 10 Questions Before Buying an Expensive Phone

Before spending ₹70,000, ₹1 lakh or more, ask yourself:

### 1. Could I buy this phone without touching my emergency savings?

If no, stop.

### 2. Would the purchase affect my regular investments?

If yes, reconsider.

### 3. Do I have existing high-interest debt?

If yes, that usually deserves priority.

### 4. Am I buying it because I need its features?

Or because I simply want the newest model?

### 5. How long will I realistically keep it?

Be honest.

### 6. What does my current phone actually fail to do?

If the answer is "nothing," upgrading becomes harder to justify.

### 7. How much will I use the expensive features?

A 200MP camera doesn't create value if you rarely take photos.

### 8. What is the total cost after accessories and protection?

Calculate the real number.

### 9. What could this money accomplish elsewhere?

This is perhaps the most important question.

### 10. Will I still be happy with the purchase six months from now?

If you're unsure, wait.

## The 48-Hour Rule

If you're tempted by an expensive phone, don't buy it immediately.

Put the purchase on hold for 48 hours.

During those two days:

- Don't watch more launch videos.
- Don't browse reviews repeatedly.
- Don't compare colors.
- Don't look at accessory combinations.
- Don't let a countdown timer push you into a decision.

Instead, look at your finances.

If you still want the phone after the emotional excitement has faded, evaluate it again.

For particularly expensive purchases, extend the waiting period to a week.

Impulse purchases usually lose some of their urgency when you stop feeding the impulse.

## What If You Really Want the Flagship?

Then buy it.

There is no prize for always buying the cheapest smartphone.

Personal finance isn't about eliminating everything enjoyable.

If you've:

- Built adequate emergency savings
- Managed your debt
- Kept your essential expenses under control
- Continued investing
- Planned for major financial goals
- And can comfortably absorb the phone's cost

then buying an expensive phone can be perfectly reasonable.

The goal isn't to convince everyone to buy a ₹20,000 smartphone.

The goal is to make sure your ₹1 lakh phone doesn't become the reason your finances are constantly under pressure.

## A Better Definition of "Affordable"

A phone is affordable when you can buy it **without compromising the things that matter more to your financial life**.

That's a much better definition than:

"Because I can pay the EMI."

The Indian smartphone market is moving toward more expensive devices, and consumers themselves are increasingly willing to stretch their budgets. Counterpoint's 2026 survey found that 46% of prospective buyers were willing to stretch for their preferred phone as prices rose, while 29% planned to stay within budget or consider refurbished devices.

That tells us something important.

Consumers don't necessarily need cheaper phones.

They need **better value decisions**.

## Final Verdict: Don't Let the Phone Decide Your Budget

The most expensive phone you can technically buy isn't necessarily the phone you can comfortably afford.

Before buying a premium smartphone, work backward from your life rather than forward from the phone.

Start with your income.

Subtract essential expenses.

Account for debt.

Protect your emergency savings.

Continue your long-term investments.

Then see what remains.

If a ₹1 lakh phone fits comfortably inside that picture, enjoy it.

If it doesn't, there's no shame in buying a cheaper model, waiting for a discount, choosing last year's flagship or keeping your current phone for another year.

In fact, sometimes the smartest smartphone upgrade is **not upgrading at all**.

A phone should improve your life.

It shouldn't quietly take control of your finances.
