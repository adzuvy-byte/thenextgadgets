If you've noticed a bright glow around subtitles, street lights, stars, or other small objects when watching a dark scene, you may be wondering: **why does my TV have blooming?** The effect can look like a soft halo spreading from a bright object into the surrounding black area. It is particularly noticeable in a dark room, and it is most commonly associated with LED and Mini LED TVs that use local dimming to improve contrast.

Blooming does not necessarily mean your TV is defective. In many cases, it is a limitation of how an LCD television controls its backlight. The good news is that you can often make it less noticeable by changing the local-dimming setting, adjusting your viewing environment, or using a picture mode that handles dark scenes more naturally.

The important part is understanding **why blooming happens in the first place**, because simply turning up or down the TV's brightness won't necessarily solve it.

## What Is TV Blooming?

TV blooming is the visible glow or halo that can appear around a bright object against a dark background.

Imagine watching a movie at night. There is a black sky with a single bright moon in the middle of the screen. Ideally, the moon should be bright while the surrounding sky remains almost completely black.

On an LCD TV with local dimming, however, the backlight is divided into zones rather than being controlled independently for every individual pixel.

If the moon occupies only part of one of those zones, the TV may have to illuminate the entire zone to make the moon bright.

The result can be a grayish or whitish glow surrounding the moon.

That's blooming.

You may also notice it around:

- White subtitles
- Movie credits
- Stars
- Street lights
- Car headlights
- Bright windows
- Small highlights in HDR movies
- White text on black backgrounds
- Bright objects moving across dark scenes

The effect is sometimes called **haloing**. The terminology varies, although modern TV testing often treats haloing and blooming as closely related manifestations of imperfect local-dimming control.

## Why Does My TV Have Blooming?

The simplest answer is:

**Your TV cannot control the backlight as precisely as the image on the screen requires.**

An LCD TV needs a light source behind the panel. Local dimming attempts to divide that light source into sections and adjust each section according to what's happening in the image.

The problem is that the zones are much larger than individual pixels.

A scene might contain a tiny bright object surrounded by a huge area of black. The TV has to make a compromise:

**Make the bright object visible → keep the surrounding area dark.**

If the dimming zone is larger than the bright object, some light spills into areas that should appear darker.

That's what your eyes perceive as blooming.

Local dimming can dramatically improve contrast compared with a TV that leaves the entire backlight illuminated, but it isn't perfect. RTINGS notes that even modern LED and Mini LED TVs can produce blooming or distracting transitions when local-dimming zones cannot precisely follow the image.

## How Local Dimming Causes Blooming

To understand blooming, it helps to understand local dimming.

A traditional LCD/LED TV has a backlight behind the LCD panel. The LCD layer controls how much of that light reaches your eyes.

A local-dimming TV adds another layer of control.

Instead of treating the entire backlight as one light source, the TV divides it into multiple zones.

For example, a simplified TV might have:

**Zone 1 | Zone 2 | Zone 3 | Zone 4**

The processor analyses the picture and tells each zone how bright or dark it should be.

If most of the screen is dark but a bright object appears in one zone, the TV can dim the surrounding zones while keeping the zone containing the highlight brighter.

That produces much better contrast.

But there is a catch.

The TV cannot normally tell one tiny section of a zone to be bright and another section of the same zone to be completely dark.

So if a bright subtitle sits inside a large dimming zone, part of that zone has to remain illuminated.

**That illuminated area is what creates the halo.**

## Why Blooming Is More Noticeable in Dark Rooms

Blooming isn't necessarily worse just because the TV is producing more light.

Your viewing environment matters.

In a dark room, your eyes become more sensitive to small differences in black levels. A faint gray glow that you might barely notice during the day can become obvious when all the lights are switched off.

That's why you may experience this:

**Daytime:** "There's hardly any blooming."

**At night:** "Why is there a giant glow around every subtitle?"

The TV hasn't necessarily changed dramatically. Your eyes and the surrounding environment have changed.

This is also why blooming can be particularly distracting during movies watched in a completely dark room.

## Does Mini LED Prevent Blooming?

No.

This is an important misconception.

**Mini LED can reduce blooming, but it does not automatically eliminate it.**

Mini LED TVs use much smaller LEDs in the backlight, allowing manufacturers to create more independently controlled areas. More precise backlight control can reduce the amount of light spreading into neighboring dark areas.

But Mini LED still uses a backlight and local dimming.

It isn't pixel-level light control.

RTINGS notes that Mini LED's benefits depend heavily on dimming-zone precision and the algorithms controlling those zones. Simply having a large number of LEDs doesn't guarantee excellent blooming performance.

This is an important point when comparing Mini LED televisions.

Don't assume:

**More Mini LEDs = No blooming**

Instead, think:

**Better backlight control + more precise zones + better processing = potentially less blooming.**

## More Dimming Zones Usually Help — But They're Not Everything

TV manufacturers often advertise the number of dimming zones as an important specification.

And it does matter.

Imagine two TVs.

### TV A

100 local-dimming zones.

### TV B

1,000 local-dimming zones.

If both TVs are the same size and have similarly good processing, TV B has much finer control over the backlight.

A small bright object is less likely to force a large area of the screen to remain illuminated.

But zone count isn't the whole story.

The processor controlling those zones matters too.

A TV with many zones can still produce distracting blooming if its dimming algorithm responds poorly to moving objects or bright highlights.

RTINGS' current testing methodology specifically separates zone precision and zone transitions, noting that algorithms can be as important as the number of zones.

So when buying a Mini LED TV, don't judge it solely by a manufacturer's advertised zone count.

## Why Do I See Blooming Around Subtitles?

Subtitles are one of the easiest ways to spot blooming.

That's because subtitles create exactly the situation that local dimming struggles with:

**Small, extremely bright objects surrounded by darkness.**

Imagine a white subtitle line across a black movie scene.

The letters themselves might occupy only a small portion of the screen, but the TV's backlight zone underneath them is much larger.

The zone needs to remain bright enough to illuminate the white letters.

That means some of the surrounding black area receives backlight illumination as well.

You see this as a glow around or above the subtitles.

This doesn't necessarily mean anything is wrong with the panel.

It is a consequence of the difference between:

**pixel-level image information**

and

**zone-level backlight control.**

## Why HDR Can Make Blooming More Noticeable

HDR content can make blooming easier to notice because HDR is designed to produce much brighter highlights alongside darker areas.

Think about a scene containing:

- A bright street lamp
- A dark street
- A black sky

The lamp might be extremely bright while almost everything around it is dark.

That is exactly the type of scene that tests a TV's local-dimming system.

Some TVs offer highlight or brightness settings that make bright areas more aggressive. While this can increase HDR impact, it can also make blooming more visible.

RTINGS similarly notes that increasing highlight brightness can come with more visible blooming around small light sources.

This is one reason why the brightest possible picture isn't always the best-looking picture.

## How to Reduce Blooming on Your TV

You usually can't completely eliminate blooming on an LCD or Mini LED TV, but you can often reduce how noticeable it is.

### 1. Lower the Local Dimming Setting

This is the first setting I'd check.

Depending on the brand, it may be called:

- Local Dimming
- Local Contrast
- Contrast Enhancer
- Adaptive Picture
- Quantum Matrix
- Full Array Local Dimming
- Precision Dimming
- Dynamic Contrast

The exact terminology varies.

If your TV offers **Low, Medium and High** local-dimming levels, try Medium first.

Higher local-dimming settings can produce deeper blacks and stronger contrast, but they can also make halos more noticeable on some TVs. RTINGS recommends reducing local-dimming intensity when aggressive dimming creates distracting haloing.

### 2. Try a Different Picture Mode

If you're using a very bright or dynamic picture mode, try:

- Movie
- Cinema
- Filmmaker Mode
- Professional
- Custom

These modes are often designed to produce a more controlled image rather than simply maximizing brightness.

Your TV may behave differently depending on the picture mode because local-dimming behavior, contrast processing and brightness targets can change.

### 3. Don't Max Out Every Brightness Enhancement

Features designed to make the picture look brighter can sometimes exaggerate blooming.

Try disabling or reducing features such as:

- Dynamic Contrast
- Contrast Enhancer
- Black Enhancer
- Peak Brightness
- Live/Dynamic Picture
- AI picture enhancements

Don't change everything at once.

Adjust one setting at a time so you know what actually made the difference.

### 4. Reduce Ambient Reflections

This won't technically remove blooming from the panel, but it can make it less distracting.

A small amount of ambient light can make the surrounding black areas appear less stark to your eyes.

This is one reason some viewers find blooming much less annoying in a normally lit living room than in a completely dark home theater.

### 5. Check the Same Scene With Local Dimming Off

This is a useful diagnostic test.

Turn local dimming off temporarily and play the same dark scene.

You may notice that blooming disappears or changes substantially, but the entire screen may become more gray because the backlight is no longer selectively dimming dark areas.

That's the trade-off.

**Less blooming can mean worse black levels.**

This is why simply turning local dimming off isn't necessarily a "fix."

## Should You Turn Local Dimming Off?

Usually, no.

If your TV has effective local dimming, switching it off can make dark scenes look flatter and gray.

Local dimming exists specifically to improve contrast by reducing the backlight behind darker parts of the image.

A better approach is to experiment with the available levels.

For example:

**High → Medium → Low**

Watch the same dark scene each time.

You may find that Medium gives you the best compromise between:

- Black levels
- HDR brightness
- Shadow detail
- Blooming

There isn't one setting that looks best on every TV.

## Can Blooming Be Fixed With Software?

Sometimes it can be reduced.

It usually cannot be completely eliminated.

Manufacturers can improve local-dimming behavior through firmware and image-processing algorithms. Better processing can help the TV determine which zones should brighten or dim and how quickly they should respond.

But software cannot turn a 100-zone backlight into a pixel-level lighting system.

There are physical limitations.

If the backlight zone is large and contains a tiny bright object, the TV still has to illuminate that zone to some degree.

So be suspicious of claims that a software update will completely eliminate blooming from an LCD TV.

It may improve the behavior.

It cannot change the fundamental architecture of the display.

## Is Blooming a Defect?

Usually, **no**.

Some degree of blooming can be normal on LCD TVs with local dimming.

The key question is how severe it is.

### Normal behavior can include:

- A mild glow around subtitles
- Some haloing around stars
- Visible light around bright objects in very dark scenes
- More blooming at aggressive local-dimming settings

### More concerning behavior includes:

- Large bright patches across the screen
- Uneven backlight visible during normal content
- Bright areas that remain after the scene changes
- Severe light leakage across large parts of the screen
- Problems that appear even with local dimming disabled

If you're seeing significant bright patches during ordinary scenes rather than only around isolated highlights, it may be worth checking the TV's warranty or service documentation.

## Blooming vs Backlight Bleeding: They're Not the Same

These terms are often confused.

### Blooming

Blooming is usually associated with local dimming.

You see a halo around a bright object because the backlight zone containing that object cannot become selectively dark around it.

### Backlight bleeding

Backlight bleed is more generally associated with unwanted light escaping around the edges or corners of an LCD panel.

You might notice bright patches near the corners even when the picture itself doesn't contain a bright object there.

### Clouding

Clouding refers to uneven-looking gray or bright patches across darker portions of the screen.

It can be more visible when displaying a uniform dark image.

These problems can look similar in a dark room, but their causes are different.

## Blooming vs Dirty Screen Effect

Dirty screen effect, or DSE, is another issue that gets confused with blooming.

DSE generally appears as uneven patches or bands during large areas of uniform color, particularly:

- Football fields
- Hockey ice
- Sky
- Snow
- Panning shots
- Large gray backgrounds

Blooming is usually tied to bright objects against dark backgrounds.

If the problem follows bright objects or subtitles, think **blooming**.

If the problem looks like stationary dirty patches across the screen, you're more likely looking at **panel uniformity or DSE**.

## Do OLED TVs Have Blooming?

Traditional OLED TVs don't suffer from LCD-style backlight blooming because OLED pixels generate their own light.

Each pixel can be controlled independently.

If a pixel needs to display black, it can turn off instead of relying on a backlight zone to become dark.

That's one of the fundamental reasons OLED TVs can produce extremely deep blacks and precise contrast. Samsung describes OLED as a self-emissive technology in which individual pixels can control their own light, while Mini LED TVs rely on a backlight with local dimming.

LG similarly describes OLED as a self-lit technology, while Mini LED uses smaller backlight LEDs and local dimming to improve LCD contrast.

That doesn't mean OLED is automatically better for everyone.

Mini LED TVs can offer substantially higher brightness and can be excellent for bright rooms and HDR viewing.

The trade-off is that an LCD/Mini LED TV still has to manage a backlight.

## Why Don't Manufacturers Just Use More Dimming Zones?

They increasingly do.

Mini LED technology allows manufacturers to pack many more LEDs into the backlight and create more precise control.

But there are several challenges.

More zones mean:

- More LEDs
- More control hardware
- More processing requirements
- More complex algorithms
- Higher manufacturing costs
- More difficult thermal and power management

And more zones alone don't solve every problem.

The processor still needs to determine how aggressively each zone should respond to the content.

A very aggressive algorithm might produce excellent black levels but visible transitions or halos.

A conservative algorithm might reduce artifacts but leave blacks looking brighter.

TV manufacturers are constantly balancing these competing goals.

## Does Screen Size Affect Blooming?

It can.

A larger screen doesn't automatically mean more blooming, but screen size, zone density and viewing distance can change how visible the effect is.

Imagine two TVs with the same number of dimming zones.

On the larger television, each zone covers a larger physical area.

That can make precise control more difficult.

This is another reason you shouldn't compare zone counts without considering the screen size and actual implementation.

## Does Viewing Distance Make Blooming Less Noticeable?

Yes, potentially.

The closer you sit, the easier it is to notice small artifacts.

Move farther away and the halo may become less obvious.

This doesn't mean the TV is producing less blooming. You're simply changing how large the artifact appears in your field of view.

For normal living-room viewing, mild blooming that looks obvious from two feet away may be barely noticeable from a typical sofa distance.

## Should You Return a TV Because of Blooming?

Not necessarily.

Before returning it, test it properly.

Use several different scenes rather than a completely black test screen.

Try:

1. A dark movie scene with subtitles
2. A night scene with street lights
3. A star field
4. HDR content
5. Normal daytime programming
6. Sports
7. Gaming

Then change the local-dimming setting.

If the blooming is visible only in a few extreme scenes and doesn't bother you during normal viewing, it may simply be a limitation you're willing to accept.

If it is distracting throughout your normal viewing, the TV may not be the right choice for your viewing environment.

## How to Test Blooming Before Buying a TV

If you're shopping for a new TV, don't rely entirely on the manufacturer's marketing claims.

If possible, look for independent testing that evaluates:

- Local-dimming performance
- Zone transitions
- Black uniformity
- HDR brightness
- Haloing
- Contrast
- Viewing angles

Also watch the TV in a dim environment.

A showroom with bright overhead lights is a poor place to judge black-level performance.

Ask to see a dark movie scene rather than only colorful demo footage.

And don't assume that a TV advertised as "Mini LED" will automatically have excellent blooming control. Mini LED implementations vary considerably between models.

## What Should You Look for If You Hate Blooming?

If blooming is one of your biggest concerns, prioritize:

### OLED

Best suited to viewers who want pixel-level black control and watch a lot of movies in darker environments.

### High-quality Mini LED

A good choice if you want high brightness and strong HDR performance but also want better black levels than a conventional LED TV.

### Strong local-dimming performance

Don't judge solely by the number of zones.

Independent testing of actual zone precision and transitions is more useful than a marketing number.

### Good image processing

The TV needs to decide when and how aggressively individual zones should brighten and dim.

Better processing can make a major difference.

## Is Mini LED or OLED Better for Avoiding Blooming?

If your only priority is avoiding traditional backlight blooming, OLED has a fundamental advantage because it doesn't use an LCD backlight divided into dimming zones.

But that doesn't automatically make OLED the right TV for every room.

Mini LED can offer:

- Higher brightness
- Strong HDR highlights
- Large-screen options
- Good performance in bright rooms
- Excellent contrast compared with conventional LCD TVs

OLED offers:

- Pixel-level light control
- Extremely deep blacks
- No traditional backlight blooming
- Excellent contrast in dark scenes

The choice depends on what you value.

## The Bottom Line

So, **why does my TV have blooming?**

Because your TV's backlight is controlled in zones rather than at the individual-pixel level. When a small bright object appears inside a much larger dimming zone, the TV has to keep part of that zone illuminated. Some of that light reaches areas that should look black, creating the halo or glow you see around the object.

Mini LED can reduce the problem by providing smaller LEDs and more precise backlight control, but it cannot guarantee zero blooming. The quality of the local-dimming algorithm matters just as much as the number of zones.

Before assuming your TV is faulty, try reducing local dimming, switching to a more accurate picture mode and testing the same scenes in different lighting conditions.

And if you're buying a new TV specifically because blooming bothers you, don't choose based on the words **"Mini LED"** or a large advertised zone count alone.

Look at the actual picture-quality performance.

Because when it comes to blooming, **how a TV controls its backlight matters more than what the marketing box calls it.**

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is blooming on a TV normal?

Some blooming can be normal on LED and Mini LED TVs that use local dimming. A small amount of haloing around bright objects in dark scenes does not necessarily indicate a defective panel.

### How do I stop blooming on my TV?

You usually cannot eliminate blooming completely on an LCD or Mini LED TV. Try lowering the local-dimming setting, switching to Movie or Cinema mode, and reducing aggressive contrast or highlight-enhancement features.

### Does Mini LED eliminate blooming?

No. Mini LED can significantly improve backlight control, but it still relies on zones rather than individual pixels. The number of zones and the quality of the TV's processing both affect blooming.

### Does OLED have blooming?

OLED does not have traditional LCD-style backlight blooming because each pixel produces its own light and can be switched off independently.

### Why is blooming worse with subtitles?

Subtitles are small, bright objects surrounded by dark areas. Because the backlight operates in larger zones, the TV may illuminate a larger area than the subtitle itself, creating a visible halo.

### Should I turn local dimming off to fix blooming?

Turning local dimming off can reduce some blooming, but it can also make blacks look gray and reduce contrast. A lower local-dimming setting is often a better compromise.

### Is blooming the same as backlight bleeding?

No. Blooming is generally a halo around bright objects caused by local-dimming limitations. Backlight bleeding is unwanted light that can appear around the edges or corners of an LCD panel.

### Do more dimming zones mean less blooming?

Usually, more zones can provide finer backlight control, but zone count alone doesn't determine performance. The algorithm controlling those zones is also important.
